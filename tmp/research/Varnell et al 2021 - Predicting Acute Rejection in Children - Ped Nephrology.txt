Predicting acute rejection in children, adolescents, and young adults
with a kidney transplant by assessing barriers to taking medication
Charles D. Varnell Jr1,2,3
& Kristin L. Rich3,4 & Bin Zhang3,5 & Adam C. Carle2,6 & Ahna L. H. Pai3,4 &
Avani C. Modi3,4 & David K. Hooper1,2,3
Received: 8 September 2020 /Revised: 15 December 2020 /Accepted: 12 January 2021
# IPNA 2021
Abstract
Background Nonadherence to immunosuppression is common among pediatric, adolescent, and young adult kidney transplant
recipients and a leading cause of graft loss. Assessing barriers to medication adherence in clinical practice may identify patients at
risk for rejection and provide therapeutic targets.
Methods Kidney transplant patients and/or their caregivers were assessed for 14 barriers to medication adherence using the
barriers assessment tool. We compared rejection rates between patients with at least one reported adherence barrier to those
without reported adherence barriers using a Kaplan-Meier estimator and Cox proportional hazard models to adjust for other
mediators of acute rejection at 2 years following barriers assessment.
Results Ninety-eight patients were assessed for barriers to adherence. Over the 2-year observation period, 22 patients developed
biopsy-proven acute rejection (BPAR). Kaplan-Meier estimates show that patients with an identified barrier to adherence were
more likely to have BPAR (p = 0.02) than patients without an identified barrier in the 24 months following barriers assessment.
The median time to rejection for patients who experienced acute rejection was 175.5 days (IQR 63–276 days) from the time of
barriers assessment. An identified barrier to adherence remained the only statistically significant predictor of BPAR with Cox
modeling (HR 2.6, p = 0.04), after accounting for age, sex, and race.
Conclusions Pediatric and adolescent kidney transplant recipients with identified adherence barriers are at increased risk for acute
rejection. Barriers to adherence provide a potentially modifiable therapeutic target that can be assessed in clinic to guide targeted
interventions.

While the issues related to long-term allograft survival are
complex, suboptimal adherence to the immunosuppression
regimen following kidney transplantation is common—
particularly in adolescents and young adults—and leads to
increased incidence of acute rejection [2], a major cause of
allograft failure [1]. Reducing acute rejection episodes has
been shown to decrease chronic rejection and improve al-
lograft survival [1]. This leads to increased life expectancy
[3] and quality of life [4], with significantly lower health
care costs [3] compared to dialysis. Therefore, medication
adherence is a potentially modifiable risk factor for inter-
ventions to improve long-term graft survival in post-
transplant patients. Unfortunately, physicians have limited
training and ability to accurately identify nonadherence [5,
6] and associated barriers to taking immunosuppressive med-
ications despite physician recognition that nonadherence is
both common and an important factor in clinical outcomes
[7, 8].
Adolescence is a particularly vulnerable period for
nonadherence due to less parental supervision, feelings of in-
vincibility, lack of fully developed executive functioning
skills, peer pressure, and increased vulnerability to risk taking
[9–11]. Children, adolescent, and young adult patients and
their caregivers often experience barriers to taking/
administering their immunosuppressive medication, in-
cluding disliking the taste, forgetting, difficulty swallowing
pills, and competing demands [2, 12]. Identifying these
barriers is a necessary first step for any effort to improve
adherence behaviors [13]. In the research setting, barriers to
taking medication have been linked to adherence across
multiple chronic conditions, including solid organ trans-
plant, diabetes, and epilepsy [10, 14–16]. In solid organ
transplant recipients, specific adherence barriers endorsed
by parents have been associated with missed doses or er-
ratic drug levels, whereas specific adherence barriers en-
dorsed by patients are associated with rejection episodes
and/or patient death [16]. In addition, adherence barriers
are more strongly linked to particular outcomes in specific
age groups (e.g., adolescents who disliked medication taste
had lower medication adherence rates) [10].
While studies have demonstrated the value of screening for
adherence barriers in the research setting, there are few studies
examining implementation of adherence barrier screening in
clinical care [12]. Our kidney transplant program standardized
and integrated screening for adherence barriers as part of stan-
dard post-transplant care using the barriers assessment tool
(BAT), an easily administered tool (< 1 min) for use in the
clinic setting [12]. The BAT was developed based on existing
validated research tools, such as the Parent Medication
Barriers Scale (PMBS)/Adolescent Medication Barriers
Scale (AMBS) [17], Cystic Fibrosis and Asthma Barriers to
Adherence Interview [15], and Pediatric Epilepsy Medication
Self-Management Questionnaire [18]. While these existing
tools are well suited and proven in the research setting and
occasional clinical use, our busy clinical setting and lack of
additional resources to dedicate to administering a barriers
tool in clinic required the creation of a new tool that could
be easily understood and completed by patients and/or their
caregivers. Once completed, the tool also needed to be easily
scored and interpreted by the clinician as part of a routine
clinic visit. To this end, we used a checklist format (i.e., yes/
no) with a limited number of known adherence barriers that
can be easily reviewed and interpreted in the clinic by any
provider [12]. The aim of this study was to determine whether
adherence barriers identified using this tool would predict re-
jection in the clinical setting after accounting for other known
risk factors for rejection. We hypothesized that patients who
endorsed at least one adherence barrier via either patient or
caregiver report would be more likely to experience biopsy-
proven acute rejection (BPAR) compared to patients with no
reported adherence barriers.
Methods
We performed a prospective cohort study to compare inci-
dence of acute rejection in patients who identified a barrier
to taking their immunosuppressive medications and those who
did not. We followed each patient for 24 months after com-
pleting the BAT or until they experienced acute rejection. This
study was approved by the site’s Institutional Review Board.
Assessment of adherence barriers
Our group previously described the development of the BAT
and our process of reliably administering it as standard-of-care
for all patients in our kidney transplant program [12]. The
BAT is a 14-item checklist that asks “what gets in the way
of taking your immunosuppression” and elicits responses that
correspond to barriers most commonly identified in the pedi-
atric adherence literature related to logistical issues (e.g., for-
getting, inconvenience), ingestion difficulties (e.g.,
swallowing, taste), efficacy (e.g., feel I don’t need it), financial
difficulties, regimen characteristics (e.g., too many medica-
tions, side effects), and patient-specific issues (e.g., refusal
by child, embarrassment) [12]. It is completed during clinic
intake in under 60 s. For patients ≥10 years old, both the
patient and their caregiver (if present) are asked to complete
the BAT. If the patient was < 10 years old, only the caregiver
is asked to complete the BAT. All kidney transplant patients
seen across six outpatient clinic locations are eligible to com-
plete the BAT. In clinical practice, patients complete the BAT
every 6 months. For this study, the first BAT completed by a
patient and/or their caregiver from January 2015 through
August 2016 was used for analysis. During the study period,

Diagnosis of acute rejection and biopsies
All patients for whom a BAT was completed were followed
and assessed for the development of BPAR from the time of
BAT assessment for 24 months. The time of BAT assessment
was chosen as the starting point for rejection risk assessment
because we hypothesized that the information gathered from
barriers assessment is relevant to subsequent rejection risk. At
our center, biopsies for kidney transplant patients are “for
cause” and indicated when there is kidney dysfunction (e.g.,
elevated creatinine), de novo donor-specific antibodies
(DSAs), new proteinuria, and/or new hypertension. The pres-
ence or absence of a barrier to adherence did not factor into the
decision to perform a kidney biopsy. Each biopsy was read by
one of two kidney transplant pathologists who were blinded to
the study and scored according to Banff classification. The
presence or absence of biopsy-proven acute cellular (≥Banff
1a) [19] or antibody-mediated rejection [20] during the obser-
vation period was the primary outcome measure. Since our
center does not perform protocol biopsies, we sought to eval-
uate for ascertainment bias by analyzing the overall number of
biopsies in the population during the observation period, the
percentage of biopsies performed in those who did and did not
endorse an adherence barrier, and the percentage of biopsies
that showed rejection between patients who did and did not
endorse an adherence barrier.
Procedure
All kidney transplant patients who had BAT data (self- and/or
caregiver-report) between January 2015 and August 2016
were included in the study. If either the patient and/or the
caregiver identified a barrier, the patient was noted to have
an adherence barrier. There were no exclusion criteria. For
patients over the age of 10 years who had developmental
delay, only their caregiver completed the BAT. All patients
cared for in our kidney transplant clinic were asked to compete
the BAT during this time as part of routine clinical care.
Electronic health records were reviewed to identify baseline
demographic (age, sex, race) and medical information/
markers of immunologic status (cause of kidney disease, cal-
culated panel reactive antibodies (cPRA) prior to transplant,
type of transplant, transplant episode, HLA mismatch, induc-
tion immunosuppression, presence of delayed graft function
(DGF) post-transplant, primary immunosuppression, prior re-
jection episodes, the presence of HLA DSAs prior to BAT
assessment, development of de novo HLA DSAs after BAT
assessment, biopsy rates, and BPAR) for the 24-month period
following the BAT assessment. DSAs were assessed using the
Luminex® microbead platform prior to transplant and at 3, 6,
and 12 months post-transplant and annually thereafter. A pre-
transplant MFI cutoff of 1500 and post-transplant MFI cutoff
of 1000 are considered positive. Due to the sample size and
uncommon outcome event of acute rejection, we did not ana-
lyze whether the number of barriers for a given patient or
specific barriers was linked to acute rejection.
Statistical analysis
Descriptive analysis was performed to describe the distribu-
tion of the variables of interest. Two-sample t tests or
Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used to compare continuous
variables of patients who identified an adherence barrier to
those that did not. Categorical variables were compared using
chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. The Kaplan-Meier esti-
mate was used to illustrate the difference in survival curves of
time to rejection between two groups. Cox proportional haz-
ard models were used to evaluate the correlation between time
to rejection and an identified adherence barrier adjusted for
key variables a priori selected based on reported risk factors in
the literature known to affect rejection (i.e., age, sex, race)
[21–27], as well as by other patient characteristics. Model
selection was based on stepwise criterion specifying that
age, sex, and race were included in the Cox model. All anal-
yses were performed using SAS version 9.4. A p value of less
than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Endorsement of barriers to adherence
Twenty-six (43%) patients and 28 (34%) caregivers identified
at least one barrier to adherence, representing 42 unique pa-
tients (43% of the cohort). No significant differences were
noted between those who did or did not endorse adherence
barriers with respect to age, sex, race, transplant type (living
vs. deceased), whether the patient had a prior transplant, prior
rejection episode, and pre-existing or de novo DSA. However,
the most common cause of CKD 5 among those with an ad-
herence barrier was genetic (15/42, 36%), whereas congenital
anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) was most
common among those without an adherence barrier (33/56,
59%) (p = 0.02).
Analysis of rejection risk
Twenty-two patients (22%) experienced acute cellular rejec-
tion (TCMR) and/or antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) dur-
ing the follow-up period, while 76 patients (78%) did not have
any acute rejection. Eighteen patients had a single diagnosis of
TCMR: 12 patients with Banff 1A, 5 patients with Banff 1B,
and 1 patient with Banff 2A, while 2 patients had a single
diagnosis of acute AMR. Two patients had mixed rejection,
1 patient with Banff 1A TCMR and acute AMR and 1 patient
with Banff 1B TCMR and acute AMR. Kaplan-Meier survival
curve (Fig. 1) displays the “rejection free probability” for pa-
tients with an identified barrier and patients without an iden-
tified barrier in the 24 months after BAT assessment. The
patients with an identified adherence barrier were more likely
to experience acute rejection than patients without an iden-
tified barrier (p = 0.02). The median time to rejection for
patients who experienced acute rejection was 175.5 days
(IQR 63–276 days) from the time of barriers assessment.
Cox modeling was performed to account for other known
risk factors for acute rejection. After model selection in-
cluding age, sex, and race, the only significant covariate
was an identified barrier to adherence. In this analysis, pa-
tients with an adherence barrier were 2.6 times more likely
to develop subsequent BPAR in the 24 months after BAT
assessment compared to patients without an adherence bar-
rier (p = 0.04) (Table 2).
Biopsies
Forty-six patients (47%) in our sample received a kidney bi-
opsy. For patients with an adherence barrier, 24 (57%) were
biopsied compared to 22 (39%) of patients without an adher-
ence barrier (p = 0.08). When evaluating the percentage of
biopsies with rejection in those who did and did not endorse
an adherence barrier, we did not find a difference between the
two groups. In this sub-analysis, 14 of 24 biopsies in patients
(58%) with an adherence barrier had acute rejection, while 8
of 22 biopsies in patients (36%) without an adherence barrier
had acute rejection (p = 0.14).

Discussion
In this study, patients with an identified adherence barrier had
a 2.6-fold increased risk of subsequently developing BPAR in
the following 24 months. This finding remained after
adjusting for other known factors known to be associated with
acute rejection, such as age, sex, and race. Our sub-analysis of
rejection rates suggests an equal number of biopsies being
performed in those with and without barriers, suggesting there
was no ascertainment bias (e.g., finding rejection in the group
with barriers because more biopsies were performed); howev-
er, we acknowledge that bias cannot be completely eliminated
and have made an earnest effort to minimize it in this analysis.
Overall, these data suggest that patients who have a barrier to
taking their medication identified by themselves or their care-
giver are at increased risk for rejection. A barrier to adherence
was common in this cohort with almost half of caregivers and
a third of patients identifying a barrier to adherence. Barriers
to adherence have been widely reported as being at least as
common or more common than our finding across other pe-
diatric chronic conditions [9, 28–30] Fortunately, unlike most
rejection risk factors, the assessment of adherence barriers
provides actionable data to the clinical team with regard to
why a patient may be exhibiting suboptimal adherence and
what might be done to mitigate the risk of rejection. Given
their prevalence, routine assessment and monitoring of adher-
ence barriers is indicated, especially for those who have en-
dorsed a barrier. We must balance routine assessment with
survey fatigue in clinical practice and as such, we chose to
assess every 6 months. Using clinical judgment, there may be
times when the BAT should be administered more frequently,
such as a new psychosocial stressor or significant change in
family dynamic. At the time of this study, there were no stan-
dardized responses or interventions for any given barrier and
the clinician could react to identified barriers at their discre-
tion. It is possible that this could attenuate any signal for
subsequent rejection risk; however, the fact that we still see
an increased risk for rejection when an adherence barrier is
identified is significant. Interestingly, patients with CAKUT
were half as likely to identify a barrier in this cohort compared
to other diagnoses. One possible reason for this is CAKUT
tends to be evident at birth, which means these families have
likely adjusted to the illness for a longer period of time and
potentially addressed barriers experienced early on in the dis-
ease process.
To date, there are few models in pediatric kidney transplan-
tation that identify patients at increased risk for acute rejection
[27, 31]. Prior studies utilize risk factors that are either non-
modifiable for patients and clinicians (e.g., race, sex) or diffi-
cult to prevent and treat (e.g., DGF, prior sensitization).
Additionally, prior approaches have included measures of ad-
herence based on provider perception, self-report of missed
doses, or biomarkers of immunosuppression adherence (e.g.,
coefficient of variation for immunosuppression drug levels)
[27, 31]. Unfortunately, while these markers may be useful
at identifying patients at increased risk for acute rejection and
decreased graft survival, they provide little insight or oppor-
tunity for providers to implement interventions to prevent
poor outcomes and have not been widely integrated into clin-
ical practice post-transplant. Furthermore, adherence measure-
ment that focuses on drug levels can be affected by several
variables, including fluid shifts in the early post-transplant

period, interacting medications, pharmacokinetic factors af-
fecting drug absorption or metabolism, and clinician drug
dosing behaviors [32]. Lastly, provider and self-report of ad-
herence are often erroneous or inflated [5, 6, 33]. In contrast,
patient- or caregiver-identified barriers to adherence represent
a specific therapeutic target that may be amenable to interven-
tions. For example, if a patient endorses disliking the taste of
their medication, the healthcare provider can change medica-
tions or work with pharmacy to mask the flavor and potential-
ly improve medication adherence. Assessing for adherence
barriers for solid organ transplant patients in the research lit-
erature is not a novel finding. The PMBS and AMBS are
validated instruments that have been reported extensively as
part of research studies to identify adolescent and parental
barriers to adherence [16, 17, 34–36]. What is novel is the
finding that the systematic assessment of barriers to adherence
using the BAT—a simple tool that can be used as standard of
care in clinical practice—was able to identify patients at
higher risk for rejection. Our study findings show that, when
applied systematically during routine clinical care, the BAT
provides clinicians a “behavioral biomarker” that is easily
identified, empowering clinicians with the information needed
to partner with their patients to address challenges and behav-
iors patients and their caregivers are experiencing.
The TAKE-IT study [13], a randomized clinical trial to
improve adherence in pediatric kidney transplantation, sup-
ports the idea of assessing and addressing adherence barriers.
In this seminal clinical trial, identified adherence barriers were
addressed via “action-focused problem solving.” Patients who
received the intervention received reminders, coaching, and
problem solving around adherence barriers and demonstrated
improved adherence and timing compared to controls (e.g.,
social support and active listening). Our study adds to this
intervention literature and compliments TAKE-IT results.
Specifically, we found that adherence barriers increase the risk
of subsequent rejection while the TAKE-IT study demon-
strates the benefits of systematic and multimodal adherence
interventions to address such identified barriers.
Our results should be interpreted in light of several limita-
tions. First, this is a single-center study that may not be gen-
eralizable to other centers. Given our sample size, we could
not conduct analyses to identify whether the number of bar-
riers per patient affected the risk for rejection, or whether
particular barriers are more common for certain individuals
or more predictive of acute rejection. Likewise, we could not
determine whether caregiver-identified vs. patient-identified
barriers to adherence presented any difference in subsequent
rejection risk, although this has been described in other patient
populations [10]. An additional limitation is that we did not
measure patient medication adherence directly, so while we
hypothesize that the causal pathway of endorsing an adher-
ence barrier leading to increased risk for subsequent rejection
is due to suboptimal medication adherence, we do not have
evidence to support that. However, prior literature would sup-
port these important relationships [16]. Clinicians were not
blinded to the results of the barriers assessment in the clinic
and were able to address identified barriers at their discretion,
so our findings may be an underestimate of the true risk of
rejection in those who identify a barrier to taking their medi-
cation without intervention. We did not perform protocol bi-
opsies, so our study did not include subclinical rejection as an
outcome. Lastly, the BAT has not been comprehensively val-
idated. Future studies are needed to more fully evaluate the
psychometric properties of the BAT. Given the findings of
this study, we are in the process of conducting a comprehen-
sive validation of the BAT. The BAT is also currently being
utilized at other pediatric kidney transplant programs, so we
hope to use multicenter data to assess generalizability.
Overall, our findings indicate that patients who have an
adherence barrier identified by themselves or a caregiver are
2.6 times as likely to experience subsequent BPAR in the 24
months following BAT assessment. Notably, this risk persists
after controlling for other known risk factors for acute rejec-
tion. This feasible assessment of adherence barriers provides
valuable information and is a necessary first step toward de-
veloping and implementing interventions to reduce the burden
of acute rejection. Patients and clinicians can then partner in
overcoming identified barriers using shared decision-
making and other approaches shown to be effective [13,
37, 38]. Since this study was completed such interventions
have been designed and are being used in our transplant
clinic. These interventions include shared decision-
making tools that were created for the five most commonly
identified barriers to adherence that are reviewed by the
patient and the healthcare team prior to the patient leaving
clinic. Additional interventions include referrals to social
work for logistical and financial barriers and referral to
psychology for behavioral barriers (e.g., child oppositional
behaviors), as well as focused discussions with the
healthcare provider for barriers related to confusion or
worries about the treatment regimen. Next steps will be to
describe the effect these interventions have on our clinical
outcome of interest, acute rejection. Additional future stud-
ies should be conducted to expand barriers assessment to
other centers in order to identify which barriers are most
important for various clinical outcomes, including medica-
tion nonadherence, acute and chronic rejection, develop-
ment of DSA, decline in glomerular filtration rate, and graft
failure. Such work is underway in the Improving Renal
Outcomes Collaborative [39].
