A Cost-effectiveness Analysis of Adherence Promotion
Strategies to Improve Rejection Rates in Adolescent
Kidney Transplant Recipients
Charles D. Varnell Jr, Kristin L. Rich, Avani C. Modi, David K. Hooper,* and Mark H. Eckman*
Rationale & Objective: Nonadherence to medi-
cal regimens increases the risk of graft loss
among adolescent and young adult recipients of
kidney transplants. Interventions that improve
adherence may decrease rejection rates, but their
perceived costs are a barrier to clinical imple-
mentation. We developed a model to assess the
cost-effectiveness of an adherence promotion
strategy, the Medication Adherence Promotion
System (MAPS).
Study Design: Simulation-based. Data sources
included published articles indexed in Medline
or
referenced
in
bibliographies
of
relevant
English-language articles. Data on costs and
outcomes were taken from a single clinical
center.
Setting & Population: US adolescent patients
after their ﬁrst kidney transplant.
Intervention: Usual posttransplant care versus
usual care plus MAPS.
Outcome: Effectiveness measured in quality-
adjusted life years (QALYs) and costs measured
in 2020 US dollars.
Model, Perspective, & Timeframe: Markov state
transition decision model. We used a health care
system perspective with a lifelong time horizon.
Results: In the base-case analysis, MAPS was
more effective and less costly than usual care.
MAPS cost $9,106 per patient less than usual
care and resulted in a gain of 0.32 QALYs. In
probabilistic sensitivity analyses, MAPS was cost
saving 100% of the time. Extending results to a
program level with 100 patients, any adherence
promotion intervention similar in effectiveness to
MAPS would cost less than $50,000/QALY if the
start-up costs were <$2.5 million and annual
costs <$188,000. Strategies with costs similar to
MAPS that reduce the risk of rejection by as little
as 3% would also have similar cost-effectiveness.
Limitations: Estimates of components and costs
for MAPS were based on a single center.
Conclusions: Adherence promotion strategies
with costs similar to MAPS can be cost-effective
as long as they reduce rejection rates by at least
3%. This model can be applied to study the cost-
effectiveness of adherence promotion strategies
with varying costs and outcomes.
S
uboptimal medication adherence is a challenge across
chronic medical conditions at all stages of life.1,2 It
results in worse health outcomes and decreased quality of
life3-5 while increasing health care costs in the United
States by an estimated $100 to $300 billion.6 Suboptimal
adherence
disproportionately
affects
adolescents
and
young adults with chronic disease for whom the charac-
teristics of normal development (eg, increased indepen-
dence, risk-taking, peer inﬂuence, and changes in brain
development) adversely impact medication-taking behav-
iors.7-9 This presents a profound challenge for adolescents
and young adults with a kidney transplant for whom
adherence to immunosuppression medication is essential
to prevent allograft rejection.10-15 Forty percent of ado-
lescents and young adults exhibit suboptimal adherence,16
which is associated with a 3-fold increased risk of allograft
loss compared with other groups.17 This often results in a
return to dialysis, leading to a 30-year decline in life ex-
pectancy and substantially reduced quality of life.5,18
Beyond the clinical impact of transplant rejection, the
economic impact is staggering. Care for an adolescent or
young adult with a failed allograft is estimated to cost
between 6 and 10 times that of a patient with a func-
tioning allograft,19 and suboptimal adherence among
kidney transplant recipients is associated with a $12,840
increase in individual 3-year medical costs.20 Thus, in-
terventions that are effective at improving immunosup-
pression adherence and reducing allograft rejection have
the potential to accomplish the Institute of Healthcare
Improvement triple aim of improving health and patient
experience at lower cost.21
In recent years, 3 landmark randomized clinical trials
have
demonstrated
the
efﬁcacy
of
multicomponent
behavior-change interventions to improve medication
adherence
in
solid
organ
transplant
recipients.22-24
Although these studies were underpowered to evaluate
clinical outcomes, the authors speculated that improved
adherence is likely to decrease rates of adverse outcomes
(eg, acute rejection, development of de novo donor-
speciﬁc antibodies, and allograft failure), justifying the
cost of such interventions.23 Additionally, there is uncer-
tainty about how best to measure adherence in clinical
practice and detect whether adherence interventions lead
to improvements in meaningful clinical outcomes.
As a complement to these clinical trials, we recently
reported the implementation of a Medication Adherence
Promotion System (MAPS) used for all patients on
immunosuppression in our kidney transplant clinic.25,26

MAPS is a practice-level intervention that focuses on
identifying patients with barriers to medication adherence
and partnering with them to overcome those barriers.
Essential
components
include
(1)
electronic
health
record
(EHR)-supported
adherence
risk
screening,
(2) systematic assessment of barriers to taking medication,
(3)
patient-centered
shared
decision-making
aids
to
address identiﬁed barriers, (4) designation of team
member responsibility to address speciﬁc barriers, (5)
adherence promotion training for the clinical team, and
(6) optional electronic medication monitoring.26 MAPS
was associated with a sustained decrease in the average
acute rejection rate in our population of patients from 1.61
per 100 patient-months before MAPS implementation
to 0.88 per 100 patient-months after MAPS.26
One barrier to widespread clinical implementation of
adherence promotion strategies noted in the previously
mentioned studies is their perceived high (or unknown)
cost.27 The objective of our study was to develop a
framework
for
evaluating
the
cost-effectiveness
of
evidence-based adherence promotion strategies to reduce
kidney transplant rejection rates. We used center-based
experience with start-up and ongoing costs associated
with MAPS as a case study that can be applied to other
adherence promotion strategies in the published literature.
Given that these interventions may not have the same costs
or clinical outcomes when implemented in different set-
tings and contexts, sensitivity analyses were performed to
explore the implications of a range of costs and program
effectiveness.
Methods
We developed a decision analytic model to simulate events
and outcomes after a ﬁrst kidney transplant for a proto-
typical 15-year-old with kidney failure. We investigated 2
strategies: (1) usual care and (2) care with MAPS inte-
grated into the transplant clinic. The outcome measures
included 2020 US dollars and quality-adjusted life expec-
tancy measured in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs).
Quality-adjusted life expectancy is a measure of effective-
ness that incorporates both duration of survival, or life
expectancy, and the quality of life during those years. It is
an established metric of effectiveness in cost-effectiveness
and cost-utility analyses.28
Review of Data
Data are provided with 95% conﬁdence intervals and
clinically plausible ranges when available (Table 1). We
modeled the natural history of kidney disease after ﬁrst
transplantation using data from the published literature.
Annual excess mortality rate after kidney transplantation is
0.02/year.18
Medication
adherence
programs
may
improve
adherence
with
immunosuppression
regi-
mens23,24 and may decrease the rate of acute rejection.26
In a study of 134 pediatric patients over 4 years, inte-
grating MAPS was associated with a 45% decrease in
biopsy-proven acute rejection.26 From these data we
calculated annual rates of acute rejection under usual care
(0.19) and MAPS (0.11) and estimated that rejection rates
decrease as a function of time from transplant by 13% each
year for patients that have not experienced acute rejec-
tion.26 Because a 45% decrease in biopsy-proven acute
rejection may not be similar across varying contexts using
MAPS or other adherence promotion strategies, we
explored this parameter in deterministic sensitivity ana-
lyses. Among pediatric recipients of kidney transplants,
acute rejection is predominantly T-cell mediated (TCMR),
roughly 76%.26 In older patients, particularly in settings of
retransplantation where antibodies may have formed, the
probability of TCMR versus antibody-mediated rejection
(AMR) changes over time, with TCMR predominating
during the ﬁrst 9 months after transplantation and AMR
predominating thereafter.29 We estimated graft loss after
TCMR to be 0.04/year30 and after AMR to be 0.07/year.31
In addition, there is a lower persistent level of graft loss
(w0.01/year) that is not related to acute rejection and
may not be impacted by adherence interventions such as
MAPS.31 Among patients with graft loss, the annual rate of
second retransplantation is estimated to be 0.14/year.32
Description of Decision Model
We used Decision Maker software33 to develop a 16 state
Markov transition model, analyze decision trees, and
perform sensitivity analyses using a lifelong time horizon.
This type of decision analytic model allows us to perform
a simulation of outcomes as patients move across different
health states based on probabilistic events over time. We
considered 2 strategies (see Fig 1) for a prototypical 15-
year old pediatric kidney transplant recipient: (1) usual
care and (2) care with MAPS.
Patients in both strategies enter the Markov simulation 3
months after transplantation. With each 3-month “tick” of
the clock, patients move from 1 health state to another

of scale such that a smaller program would likely cost more
per patient for start-up whereas a larger program would
likely cost less per patient.
The outcome costs include publicly available cost
data for (1) kidney biopsies (performed as an outpatient
procedure on patients with acute rejection), (2) treatment
of TCMR and AMR, (3) dialysis care, and (4) post–kidney
transplant care. In an effort to underestimate the beneﬁts of
adherence promotion strategies, the costs for treatment of
rejection were based on the lowest intensity treatment
from the published literature. Thus, the costs for treatment
of TCMR were based on a hospitalization for 3 pulse doses
of intravenous methylprednisolone and costs for AMR were
based on a single outpatient infusion of rituximab and 4
outpatient infusions with intravenous immune globulin

with both rejection types because treatment for more
severe rejections may involve prolonged hospitalizations
for T-cell–depleting therapies such as antithymocyte
globulin and/or treatment with plasmapheresis, proteo-
some inhibition, or other novel therapies.36-38 Costs
were expressed in 2020 US dollars. Future costs and
effectiveness were discounted at 3% per year.28,39
Quality of Life
Because kidney failure has a signiﬁcant impact on quality
of life, the use of QALYs as a measure of effectiveness is
particularly important.5 Separate utilities were used for
patients with kidney failure receiving dialysis and those
post–kidney transplantation.40-42
Analyses
The base-case analysis examines the incremental cost-
effectiveness ratio (ICER) of MAPS compared with usual
care. Given the analyses are simulation-based, determin-
istic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were performed
to examine the impact of parameter uncertainty. Probabi-
listic sensitivity analyses using second-order Monte Carlo
simulations were conducted using distributions for most
parameter values in the decision model. Beta and logit
distributions were used for probabilities and lognormal
distributions for relative risks, hazard ratios, rates, and
costs. Deterministic sensitivity analyses were performed by
systematically varying 1 or more parameters values over
clinically relevant ranges.
Results
Base-Case Analysis
In the base-case analysis, we discounted costs and effec-
tiveness at 3% per year. As shown in Table 2, according to
the model MAPS cost $9,106 per patient less than usual
care and was more effective, resulting in a gain of 0.32
QALYs per patient. Without discounting, MAPS reduces
costs by $3,131 and leads to a 0.8 gain in QALYs, domi-
nating usual care. Figures S1 and S2 show movement of
the patient cohort through the major health states over
time from the start of the simulation. Patients in the usual
care strategy develop acute rejection more quickly and
have a higher peak than those in the MAPS strategy.
Figure 2 shows cumulative probability of acute rejection
and death-censored graft survival for both strategies. Fifty-
eight percent and 38%, respectively, of patients in usual
care versus MAPS experience a ﬁrst episode of acute
rejection by 10 years. Graft survival at 10 years is roughly
67% for patients in usual care and 70% for patients in
MAPS.

inates usual care by being less costly and more effec-
tive until the RR exceeds 0.88. Above a RR of 0.88
(indicating decreasing effectiveness) the ICER of the
MAPS strategy increases. Above a RR of 0.95, MAPS is

Discussion
A recent Cochrane review44 as well as a National Institutes
of Health consensus conference45 promoted the need for
randomized trials of adherence promotion interventions
for adolescent and young adult transplant recipients, and
recommended the development and testing of multicom-
ponent
interventions.
In
response,
3
randomized
controlled trials of multicomponent interventions have
demonstrated improved medication adherence in solid
organ transplant recipients.22-24 In a clinical setting,
implementation
of
a
multicomponent
adherence
intervention, MAPS, was associated with a 45% reduction
in acute rejection.26 Yet the perceived or unknown cost of
such interventions remains a barrier to clinical imple-
mentation.27 In this study, MAPS was used as a case study
for cost-effectiveness of adherence interventions to reduce
acute rejection. Analyses demonstrated that an adolescent
kidney transplant patient, 15 years of age, could gain an
average 0.32 QALYs from participating in an adherence
program with similar costs and effectiveness to those we
estimated from MAPS. This corresponds to a decrease in
acute rejection episodes from 63% to 43% over the life-
time of a patient. In addition, the average lifetime cost of
MAPS is estimated to be $9,106 less per patient than usual
care.
Although the results for our particular MAPS program
look promising, estimates for the cost-effectiveness of
MAPS come from a small, single-center study and may vary
widely in different contexts or settings. The sensitivity
analyses we performed allow our model and analysis to
apply to other medication adherence programs that might
have different costs or outcomes (ie, different effect size in
reducing rejections). Although the absolute decrease (or
absolute risk reduction) in acute rejection rates of 8.8% per
year (10.56% vs 19.32%) estimated from MAPS is both
clinically and economically signiﬁcant, sensitivity analysis
shows that if start-up and ongoing costs are similar,
adherence promotion strategies that lead to an absolute
rejection rate decrease as small as 3% per year (ie, from
20% to 17%) would be less costly and more effective than
usual care. In fact, if these strategies only decreased the rate
of rejection from 20% to 18.5% per year, they would still
be highly cost-effective based on the commonly accepted
willingness-to-pay threshold of $50,000 per QALY.28,43
The estimated start-up costs of $139.51 per patient with
ongoing annual cost of $179.53 per patient (Fig 4) pri-
marily reﬂect personnel costs along with expenditures for
equipment. Through sensitivity analysis, we ﬁnd that
adherence promotion strategies with effectiveness similar
to those estimated for MAPS would remain cost saving even if
start-up costs are as high as $9,300 per patient and annual
ongoing costs are as high as $800 per patient. Using the
data from our real-world experience, our model predicts

overall and not a particular insurer, provider, or hospital.
Thus, they represent as best as possible the true resource
utilization costs at the aggregate health system level.28
The implications of this study extend well beyond the
speciﬁc use case of MAPS and can be applied to any of the
published interventions to improve adherence in solid
organ transplant. To date, published randomized clinical
trials have not been powered to examine clinical outcomes
(eg, rejection or allograft function), so the cost savings or
cost-effectiveness of such interventions could not be
determined. A recent editorial27 highlighting these studies
acknowledged that the barriers to implementing such
programs include the lack of demonstrated effectiveness in
improving clinical outcomes and the perceived cost of
implementation in the clinical arena. As Foster noted,
“there is a belief that adherence interventions should be
able to be implemented at little or no additional cost—a
requirement that never applied to medical or surgical in-
terventions.”27 Our analysis, demonstrates that even very
costly interventions would still be highly cost-effective or
even cost saving if there was a measurable decrease in
acute rejection associated with their implementation. Thus,
clinical programs designed to reduce acute rejection
through improved medication adherence have the poten-
tial to promote the Institute for Healthcare Improvement’s
Triple Aim to (1) improve the patient experience of care
by increasing quantity and quality of life, (2) improve the
health of populations, and (3) reduce the per capita cost of
health care.21
This study has several limitations. First, the cost and
effectiveness estimates are from a small single-center
study, which may not be generalizable given that the
effectiveness of any complex intervention is related to
context-speciﬁc factors (eg, quality improvement support,
organizational support, other environmental factors). Yet,
although the speciﬁc results may not be generalizable, the
overall framework of this cost-effectiveness model should
be. The beneﬁt of sensitivity analysis is that it generalizes
the speciﬁc situation to an entire range of costs and
effectiveness of any intervention. Thus, as long as one is
