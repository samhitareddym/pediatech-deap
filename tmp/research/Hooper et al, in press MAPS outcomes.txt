A Medication Adherence Promotion System to Reduce
Late Kidney Allograft Rejection: A Quality Improvement
Study
David K. Hooper, Charles D. Varnell Jr, Kristin Rich, Adam Carle, John Huber, Farida Mostajabi, Devesh Dahale,
Ahna L.H. Pai, Jens Goebel, and Avani C. Modi
Rationale & Objective: Adolescent and young
adult kidney transplant recipients have a high risk
of rejection related to suboptimal adherence.
Multicomponent interventions improve adherence
in controlled trials, but clinical implementation is
lacking. We describe an initiative to reduce allo-
graft rejection using evidence-based adherence
promotion strategies.
Study Design: Interrupted time series.
Setting & Participants: Kidney transplant re-
cipients cared for at Cincinnati Children’s Hos-
pital ≥1 year after transplant and taking ≥1
immunosuppressive
medication(s)
from
2014
through 2017.
Quality Improvement Activities: The following
interventions, collectively called MAPS (Medica-
tion Adherence Promotion System), were imple-
mented
over
14
months:
(1)
adherence
promotion training for clinical staff, 2) electronic
health
record–supported
adherence
risk
screening,
(3)
systematic
assessment
of
medication adherence barriers, (4) designation
of speciﬁc staff to address adherence barriers,
(5) shared decision-making with the patients to
overcome
adherence
barriers,
(6)
follow-up
evaluation to assess progress, and (7) optional
electronic medication monitoring.
Outcomes: Primary Outcome: Late acute rejec-
tion. Process measures were conducted to
assess barriers, identify barriers, and perform
interventions.
The
secondary
outcomes/
balancing measures were de novo donor-speciﬁc
antibodies (DSA), biopsy rate, and rejections per
biopsy.
Analytical Approach: Time series analysis using
statistical process control evaluated patient-days
between acute rejections as well as monthly
rejections per 100 patient-months before and
after
implementation.
To
control
for
known
rejection
risk
factors
including
changes
in
treatment and case mix, multivariable analyses
were performed.
Results: The monthly rejection rate fell from 1.61
rejections per 100 patient-months in the 26
months
before
implementation
to
0.88
rejections per 100 patient-months in the 22
months after implementation. In the multivariable
analysis, MAPS was associated with a 50%
reduction in rejection incidence (incidence rate
ratio, 0.50 [95% CI, 0.27-0.91]; P = 0.02). DSA
and time since transplant (per each additional
year)
were
also
associated
with
rejection
incidence (incidence rate ratio, 2.27 [P = 0.02]
and 0.87 [P = 0.02], respectively).
Limitations: Single-center study, and potential
confounding by unmeasured variables.
Conclusions:
Clinical
implementation
of
evidence-based adherence-promotion strategies
was associated with a 50% reduction in acute
rejection incidence over 2 years.

Methods
We followed the SQUIRE 2.0 guidelines19 for reporting.
The Cincinnati Children’s Hospital (CCHMC) Institutional
Review Board approved this study with a waiver of consent
(study ID: 2017-6122).
Setting
The CCHMC KT program performs 20 to 30 transplants
annually and cares for w120 posttransplant patients. After
the ﬁrst year, patients are seen in the clinic quarterly, with
monthly laboratory monitoring. Most patients receive dual
immunosuppression with tacrolimus and either myco-
phenolate mofetil or azathioprine. Patients with higher
immunologic risk also receive low-dose prednisone.
Donor-speciﬁc
antibodies
(DSAs)
are
assessed
using
Luminex assays before and after transplant at 3, 6, and 12
months and then annually. Mean ﬂuorescence intensity
cutoffs of 1,500 are considered positive. All biopsies after
1 year are “for cause.” Our team consists of 9 pediatric
nephrologists, 6 nephrology fellows, 3 transplant nurse
coordinators, and an advanced practice nurse, pharmacist,
social worker, clinical psychologist, and dietitian. Before
the
QI
intervention,
we
implemented
systems
for
recording each rejection within the electronic health re-
cord (EHR), care management by transplant nurse co-
ordinators, and EHR-assisted previsit planning where the
team reviews patients’ records before their clinic visits.
The analysis period spanned January 1, 2014, through
December 31, 2017. All patients were included who were
>1 year posttransplant and were taking at least one
immunosuppressant medication at any time during the
study period. The majority of patients were observed
during both the pre- and post-QI intervention periods, and
a single patient could contribute multiple rejections during
either phase or both. New transplant patients or patients
who transferred into the cohort were included on the date
of their ﬁrst transplant anniversary or their transfer,
whichever was later. Patients who transferred care to
another center, experienced allograft failure, developed
malignancy, or died were excluded from analysis on the
event date.
Interventions
In October 2014, after approval by clinical leadership for
this QI activity, our interdisciplinary team (including a
software engineer and former patient) convened to iden-
tify essential system components to systematically identify
and address patient barriers to taking immunosuppression
(Fig 1; Table 1). This bundle of interventions, collectively
called
the
Medication
Adherence
Promotion
System
(MAPS), includes the following: (1) adherence promotion
training for clinical team members, (2) EHR-supported
adherence risk screening,20 (3) systematic assessment of
barriers to taking medication (Fig 2),21 (4) designation of
speciﬁc team members responsible to partner with patients
to address barriers (Fig 2), (5) patient-centered shared
decision-making to develop action plans (see Table 2), (6)
planned follow-up evaluation to assess progress and adapt
action plans, and (7) optional electronic adherence
monitoring.
Implementation and Study of Interventions
Each intervention component was iteratively tested, reﬁned,
and implemented sequentially using plan-do-study-act cy-
cles, as previously described elsewhere.21 We started testing
each intervention component with 1-2 patients across 1-2
providers and then, after reﬁnement, spread the interven-
tion to additional patients and providers until it was fully
implemented as standard care for all visits. We started with
paper barriers assessments in January 2015.21 By the end of
February 2016, all MAPS components had been integrated
into clinical workﬂow care (Fig 1).
Measures
The primary outcome measure was biopsy-proven late
acute rejection, acute TCMR grade 1a or higher or AMR
according to the Banff 2013 criteria.22 There was a single
instance of fulminant kidney failure assumed to be rejec-
tion without biopsy conﬁrmation; this occurred in a pa-
tient who had not come for care in many months. Since
2011, every rejection episode was documented in discrete
ﬁelds within the EHR. Data and biopsy reports were
extracted from the EHR and were manually validated by
the ﬁrst and second authors. Biopsies with rejection within
60 days of a prior rejection in the same patient were
considered persistent rejection whereas rejections > 60
days apart were considered separate events. Because this

Patient-Level Analysis
Although SPC is an accepted method of assessing change in
system performance over time,23-28 it lacks the ability to
control for changes in case-mix risk factors (covariates)
that might also inﬂuence observed changes in outcome.
We therefore conducted analyses to examine whether the
incidence rate of rejection differed across the MAPS
implementation period after adjusting for covariates.
Because the observation time was not uniform and some
covariates varied over time for a given patient, we used a
multivariable parametric survival model29-31 rather than a
Poisson model. The hazard ratio in this model can be
interpreted as an adjusted incidence rate ratio (IRR). To
account for dependence that resulted from some patients
having repeated rejections (within and/or across both
MAPS periods), we used a model with shared frailty.29-31 A
shared frailty survival model is analogous to a random
effects regression model with a patient-speciﬁc random
intercept. We used an exponential survival distribution
because it demonstrated a better ﬁt to the data relative to
other probability distributions (eg, Weibull).
To develop a ﬁnal model with at least 5 rejection events
per covariate,32 we ﬁrst performed univariable survival
analysis with each of 12 potential covariates (Table 3).
Covariates with fewer than 5% missing values and either
an incidence rate ratio of >1.3 (or <0.77) or P < 0.1 were
included in the ﬁnal multivariable model. We also used
shared frailty, exponential survival models to evaluate
whether the incidence of biopsies, rejections per biopsy,
de novo DSA formation, and type of rejection differed
between the pre- and post-MAPS periods. The P values for
these analyses indicate whether the incidence rate for a
variable (eg, biopsies) differed across MAPS implementa-
tion periods. Stata (StataCorp) was used for all analyses,
and P < 0.05 using a 2-tailed test was considered statisti-
cally signiﬁcant.

Post-MAPS Implementation
In the 5 weeks after MAPS implementation, there were 4

22-month postimplementation period than predicted by
the prior 2 years.
Barriers Assessment and Interventions
After MAPS implementation, 418 barriers assessment tools
were completed for 99 out of 110 patients (90%) in the
post-MAPS period. Barriers were identiﬁed for 36 of 99
patients (36%) on 116 of 418 assessments (27.8%). The
most common barriers were side effects (19.8%), disliking
the taste (19.8%), and forgetting (16.4%). A total of 91
interventions were documented in 29 patients. Twenty-
eight patients were offered adherence monitoring, but 6
(21%) refused, and 6 (21%) had technical difﬁculties.
Sixteen participants (57%) successfully used adherence
monitors during the study period (median of 403.5
[range, 19-804] days).
Univariable and Multivariable Analysis
In univariable survival analysis, MAPS was the only variable
associated with decreased rejection rate, with an IRR of
0.51 (95% CI, 0.28-0.94; P = 0.03) (Table 3). DSA his-
tory, repeat KT, donor type, race, prior rejection, HLA
mismatch, and time since transplant all met the criteria for
inclusion in the ﬁnal model. None of the covariates
signiﬁcantly violated the proportionality assumption. As
shown in Table 4, after adjusting for covariates in the
multivariable model, the post-MAPS treatment period was
associated with a 50% reduction in the incidence of
rejection (IRR, 0.50 [95% CI, 0.27-0.91]; P = 0.02). DSA
history was associated with a >2-fold increase in incidence
of rejection (IRR, 2.27 [95% CI, 1.12-4.57]; P = 0.02),
and each year after transplant was associated with a 13%
decreased incidence of rejection (IRR, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.77-
0.97]; P = 0.02).

Secondary Analysis and Balancing Measures
As Table 5 shows, rate of kidney biopsies per 100 patient-
months did not differ signiﬁcantly before and after MAPS

MAPS was fully integrated as standard care in our clinic
and was applied to all patients on immunosuppression,
thus demonstrating its feasibility in a real-world setting.
Using a system/population-based analysis with statistical
process control, we observed a 45% reduction in late acute
rejection rate within months of complete MAPS imple-
mentation. This reduction was sustained for nearly 2 years,
despite no signiﬁcant change in kidney biopsy rates.
Univariable
and
multivariable
analysis
evaluating
patient-level outcomes and adjusting for important cova-
riates found that MAPS implementation was associated
with a 50% lower incidence of rejection, suggesting that
the observed decrease in late acute rejection is not
explained by known changes in case-mix. In this analysis,
both time since transplant and presence of DSA were sig-
niﬁcant predictors of rejection risk, consistent with the
literature.33 In the 22 months after MAPS implementation,
14 fewer late rejections were observed than expected based
on results from the prior 2 years.
We primarily observed a decrease in TCMR, which is
more amenable to treatment and is thought to have less
negative impact on allograft survival than AMR. However,
improvement in late acute rejection has been shown to
decrease chronic rejection and graft failure in both chil-
dren34,35 and adults,36,37 and recent literature has identi-
ﬁed deleterious effects of TCMR on long-term graft
function.3 Furthermore, treatment of rejection often re-
quires hospitalization and may include invasive proced-
ures.38 Thus, the observed decrease in late acute rejection
is likely to have an important impact on allograft survival,
health care cost, and quality of life. Although there was a
decrease in de novo DSA after MAPS implementation, it
was not statistically signiﬁcant, and no difference in AMR
was noted.
This work builds on the landmark randomized clinical
trials11,14,15 that demonstrated the efﬁcacy of multicom-
ponent interventions16 to improve medication adherence
in transplantation. These interventions included feedback
of adherence data, social support, problem solving to
overcome adherence barriers with staff members trained in
developing action plans with patients, and follow-up
evaluations to iteratively modify and improve adherence.
Although the “dose” of these adherence interventions
varied by study, all showed adherence improvements.
Notably, each study highlighted the future need to
implement such approaches in the clinical environment
and to understand impact on rejection rates.
Thus, our QI study was not designed to directly mea-
sure adherence nor to test the efﬁcacy of such in-
terventions, but rather to implement them in clinical care
and measure the associated changes in rejection rates. Our
approach builds on well-established models of behavior
change in children, adolescents, and young adults using
the key components of problem solving with trained team
members, action planning to address adherence barriers,
follow-up evaluations to modify action plans as needed,
and engagement of social support. Due to cost and

electronic monitoring. Indeed, fewer than two-thirds of
the 28 patients who were offered electronic monitoring
found it acceptable and functional in practice. This sug-
gests that further innovation is necessary before this be-
comes a widespread intervention in clinical practice.
It is informative to note the similarity in outcomes
between MAPS and TAKE-IT,11 a randomized clinical trial
of adherence interventions in adolescent KT recipients. The
TAKE-IT study was underpowered to assess rejection out-
comes, but the difference in rejection rates between the
control and treatment groups in TAKE-IT was remarkably
similar to our pre- and post-MAPS implementation rejec-
tion rates. Whereas TAKE-IT observed 1.69 rejections per
100 patient-months in the control arm compared with
1.06 in the treatment arm (P = 0.30),11 we observed a
reduction from 1.61 rejections per 100 patient-months at
baseline to 0.88 in the 22-month period after MAPS
implementation—a result that was statistically signiﬁcant
in multivariable survival analysis after controlling for other
rejection risk factors.
Several factors are likely required for MAPS to be suc-
cessful, and it is difﬁcult to identify the key ingredient.
However, the use of evidence-based adherence strategies
that foster a culture of nonjudgmental problem solving,
involvement of interdisciplinary team members with
complementary expertise, creation of algorithms to sys-
tematically assess and address adherence barriers by the
clinical team, and EHR to facilitate processes reliably all
likely contributed. To adapt this approach to other settings,
providers
must
assign
responsibility
for
addressing
adherence barriers among existing staff and empower
them with training and tools to address adherence barriers.
Work is underway to adapt and spread MAPS in KT pro-
grams throughout the United States via the Improving
Renal Outcomes Collaborative (IROC) as well as through
learning health care networks for other chronic conditions
(eg, epilepsy and rheumatology).
We note several limitations that impact the generaliz-
ability of this study. First, adherence was not directly
measured in most patients due to a lack of resources and
patient
acceptance.
However,
the
assessment
of
an
important clinical end point (eg, rejection) is a strength
that offsets this limitation. Second, the ﬁndings are from a
single center, and generalizing MAPS to other environ-
ments will require adaptation to ﬁt the local context. Third,
inference of causality in observational studies should be
undertaken with caution. Although every attempt was
made to control for known covariates, in time-series
analysis it is not possible to control for all possible con-
founders, including secular trending in outcomes, that
may inﬂuence results. Nonetheless, interrupted time series
is an appropriate methodology to evaluate the clinical
impact of interventions already shown to be effective
through clinical trials,25 and the observation of a large
treatment effect that corresponds with the timing of the
interventions, persists for 2 years, and corroborates prior
research ﬁndings (eg, TAKE-IT) all increase conﬁdence in
the conclusions. Finally, due to the relatively small sample
size and rarity of rejection, we are unable to assess which
adherence barriers or intervention were most associated
with the risk of rejection.
In summary, we adapted evidence-based adherence
interventions and reliably implemented them at the point
of care with clinical staff supported by the EHR. This
multimodal, provider-delivered system to identify and
mitigate patient and caregiver barriers to taking immu-
nosuppression was associated with a 50% reduction in late
allograft rejection. MAPS has become the standard of care
in our clinic and should be tested in other settings.
