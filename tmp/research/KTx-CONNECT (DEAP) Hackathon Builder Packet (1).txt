HACKATHON BUILDER PACKET
KTx-CONNECT (aka DEAP)
Kidney Tx Communication & Online Nudges for Nonadherence Early Capture & Tracking
The challenge
Design a way to run the MAPS loop (find a patient's medication barriers, agree on one plan,
check back) between clinic visits and at centers without a dedicated adherence team.
WHY IT MATTERS
Teens and young adults with a
kidney transplant must take anti-
rejection medicine every day for as
long as the kidney lasts. About 40%
of them struggle with this, and their
transplants fail at roughly 3 times
the rate of other age groups. A
failed transplant usually means a
return to dialysis.
WHAT ALREADY WORKS
Cincinnati Children's built MAPS
(Medication Adherence Promotion
System) as standard care in 2015–
2016. Afterward, late acute rejection
fell by about half: 1.61 to 0.88
episodes per 100 patient-months. A
cost model found MAPS saved
about $9,106 per patient and added
0.32 quality-adjusted life years.
THE PROBLEM TO SOLVE
MAPS runs on people: trained staff,
a paper or tablet checklist entered
into the chart, and a team member
who calls back within 2 weeks. Most
transplant centers cannot staff that.
Most missed doses also happen at
home, between visits, when nobody
on the care team can see them.
DESIGN QUESTION
How might we keep MAPS's barrier → plan → follow-up loop running between visits
and at any center, while keeping a human in the conversations that need one?
Any form factor is fair game: app, text messages, clinic kiosk, inside the electronic health record, a
wearable, a pharmacy touchpoint, or something we haven't thought of.
WANT TO SEE HOW WE PICTURED IT?
Our narrated concept demo is at demo.graftwisehealth.com. It shows one take: a clinician portal
plus patient and caregiver web apps. We are not committed to that form factor. Use it to understand
the clinical loop, not as a template to copy.

What must carry over, and what's open
The six functions below are where we believe MAPS gets its effect. Keep each of them, but feel free to
reinvent how each one is delivered.
MUST KEEP
WHAT IT MEANS IN PRACTICE
1. Ask about barriers, regularly and in
a structured way
The 14-item Barriers Assessment Tool (BAT): "What gets in the way of
you taking your medicine?" Today it's done every 6 months, or sooner
if something changes.
2. Hear from the patient and the
caregiver separately
Caregiver only for patients under 10; both for patients 10 and older.
Disagreement between them is a signal.
3. Give every barrier a named owner
Each barrier routes to a specific role (see the reference below). Clear
ownership is part of why MAPS worked.
4. Agree on one concrete plan per
barrier
One solution to try, with who, what, how, where and when. It's made
with the patient, not handed to them.
5. Close the loop
The owner checks back within 2 weeks: working, not working, or
adjust. Then keep checking over time (the durability phase).
6. Use risk signals already in the
chart
Flags such as tacrolimus levels that swing more than 2 SD across 4
draws, no clinic visit in over 4 months, or no labs in over 6 weeks.
The tone stays nonjudgmental throughout. Missed doses are a problem to solve together, never a failure to
punish.
Open for you to redesign
Form factor: where and how the loop happens (app, text, voice, kiosk, the health record, pharmacy,
school)
Engagement: why a 15-year-old would open it a third time
Shifting responsibility from parent to teen over several years
Triage for staff: turning between-visit data into a short, trustworthy worklist
Self-help for simple barriers (forgetting, taste) while complex ones still reach a human
Access: language, literacy, disability and families without reliable data plans
Medication monitoring: smart pillboxes have been used, but about 4 in 10 patients offered one in the
original study refused it or had technical trouble
One firm limit: motivational interviewing, the counseling technique behind the action-plan
conversation, needs a trained person. Your design can prepare that conversation and make it shorter.
It should not try to replace it.

The team also flags three things the checklist doesn't ask about: suspected health-literacy problems (to
the social worker), depression, anxiety or ADHD in the patient or parent (to psychology), and organization
or executive-function difficulties (to neuropsychological testing).
The five action plans
Shared decision-making handouts made with families. Each ends with one solution to try:
Forgetting: phone or app reminders, or pairing doses with a daily routine such as brushing teeth
Taste: pharmacist flavoring, coatings or syrups, numbing taste buds with a popsicle first, or mixing with
food when allowed
Pill swallowing: practicing with candy, changing head position, coating the pill
Embarrassment: carrying and taking medicine discreetly; a short script for explaining it to friends
Side effects: a clinician-facing guide to changing, adding or adjusting medicines
In practice, about 1 in 3 patients reports at least one barrier. The most common are side effects, bad taste
and forgetting.
